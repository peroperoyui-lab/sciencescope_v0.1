Set-Theoretic Methods for the Social Sciences

Chapter 3 · Consistency and Coverage

3.1 Set-theoretic consistency

Consistency assesses the degree to which the cases sharing a given combination of conditions agree in displaying the outcome in question. When consistency is low, there is little point in assessing coverage, since the set relation is not supported.

With fuzzy sets, consistency is computed as the sum of the minimum of each case’s membership in X and in Y, divided by the sum of memberships in X. Values below 0.75 indicate substantial inconsistency; researchers often choose a threshold of 0.8 or higher for sufficiency.

3.2 Coverage

Coverage gauges empirical relevance or importance. When there are several paths to the same outcome, the coverage of any single path may be quite small, and raw coverage should be distinguished from unique coverage.

Calibration requires the researcher to specify three qualitative anchors: full membership, full non-membership and the crossover point. These anchors should be grounded in substantive knowledge rather than in the sample distribution alone.

Truth tables are then constructed by listing all logically possible combinations of conditions and sorting cases into the rows of the table according to their membership scores.

The analysis of necessary conditions should precede the analysis of sufficiency. A condition is necessary if, whenever the outcome is present, the condition is also present; with fuzzy sets, Y must be a subset of X.

Necessity consistency is computed by dividing the sum of the minimum memberships by the sum of memberships in the outcome. A consistency of 0.9 or higher is usually required before a condition is treated as necessary.

Coverage of necessity, in turn, indicates whether the condition is trivial: a condition that is present in nearly all cases will pass the consistency test regardless of the outcome.
